Overview

How might we help students keep their habits without punishing them for slipping?

For RPI's DIS 3 course, our team of three had four months to design around a social issue. We chose motivation loss and burnout. I led the research, designed the app, and shaped the concept for a physical companion device.

Research
Talked with 20+ RPI students about why their habits break.
Reframe
Turned the moment people quit, a missed day, into a moment to reflect.
Design
A mobile app plus the Focus Block, a desk device for quiet accountability.

Problem

Habit apps punish you for slipping, so people quit.

Based on interviews and surveys with 20+ RPI students.

"Notifications are too easy to ignore. But something sitting on your desk, you can't ignore that."

That's what one RPI student told us, and it became the seed for everything. People aren't failing at habits because they don't care. They quit because the tools punish them for slipping, and starting over feels worse than stopping.

40% of the students we surveyed still rely on physical journals. That's not nostalgia. It's a signal that apps alone aren't meeting the emotional need.

Opportunity

Reflection over reset. Presence over notifications.

Two products changed how we saw the problem: TRMNL, an e-ink desk display, and LoveBox, a connected device couples use to send messages through a spinning heart. Both create a presence in your space that a push notification never will.

Reflection over reset
A missed day shouldn't erase your progress. It should become a note in your streak, not the end of it.
Presence over notifications
A notification is easy to swipe away. An object on your desk quietly stays in view.

Solution

Moti-Track: habits that survive a bad day.

A mobile app for tracking and reflection, plus the Focus Block, a desk companion that keeps your streak and your friends' streaks in view.

Physical Device
The Focus Block: always-on accountability

The Focus Block sits on your desk and shows your streak alongside your community's. No notifications. No demands. Just presence.

  • 5 screens: 4 sides and 1 top for settings
  • 3D-printed frame in PLA or PETG
  • Embedded speaker and rechargeable battery
  • Wireless charging pad on the bottom

Outcome

The professor and classmates responded positively at the final critique.

We presented Moti-Track as our final project for DIS 3. The Focus Block was never built as working hardware, so the concept still needs real-world proof. Next, I'd test two things with actual users: whether the reflection prompt reduces drop-off after a missed day, and whether a desk device can sync streaks in real time without feeling intrusive.

Research

Students don't quit because they stop caring. They quit because apps punish them.

Over two weeks, we surveyed and interviewed 20+ RPI students who were actively building habits, or had tried and given up. There was no product to test yet, so this was about understanding the emotional landscape first.

40%
still use physical journals as their primary habit tool
35%
rely on digital apps like Notion or Streaks
25%
lean on social support as their primary motivator
01

Punishing UX kills momentum faster than missing a day does

Students stopped using apps not because they missed a habit, but because of how the app responded. A wiped streak felt like being told to start from zero.

02

Notifications are too easy to ignore

One student put it directly: "I just swipe them away without thinking." A physical object works differently: ambient, persistent, with no demand for your attention.

03

People want accountability, not competition

Knowing a friend is also working on their habits, without being ranked against them, was consistently more motivating than any streak number or reward system.

04

The 40% journal stat isn't random

People who stuck with journals called them "gentler" and "more personal." That word kept coming up. It wasn't about features. It was about how the tool made them feel about themselves.

Process

We didn't start with the cube.

Early ideas like flashcards and analog note systems were too passive to touch the emotional problem we'd found. Before opening Figma, we sketched the core screens on paper. These three sketches set the direction: what happens when you miss a day, how reflection works, and what the Focus Block could be.

Key Design Decisions

Three choices that shaped the product

01

A missed day opens a prompt, not a reset

When you miss a habit, Moti-Track asks a short question like "What challenged you?" instead of wiping your streak. Your answer is saved as a note on that day, so the streak stays intact and the slip becomes part of the story.

02

A physical device, not just another app

The hardest part was defining what the cube should be. It became a connected object that shows your streak and your friends' streaks without asking you to open anything. Always visible, never demanding.

03

Community without competition

We talked about leaderboards and decided against them. People want to feel like others are in it with them, not like they're losing a race. Seeing a friend's 12-day streak is motivating. Being ranked 8th out of 10 is demoralizing.

Reflection

What I learned

01

Define the object before the screens.

The hardest part wasn't the Figma screens. It was figuring out what the cube should be. Once I saw it as ambient presence instead of an alert system, everything else followed.

02

Show the work earlier than feels comfortable.

The further into a design you get before showing it to someone, the harder it is to change direction. Next time I'd bring in outside eyes much earlier, even while the work still feels unfinished.
